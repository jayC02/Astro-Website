Domestic EPCs in Scotland

An Energy Performance Certificate shows how efficient a home is and what would improve it. Here is what it covers, when Scottish law requires one, and what happens when we visit.

Updated 18 November 2026

What an EPC shows

A domestic EPC rates a home's energy efficiency on a scale from A, the most efficient, to G, the least. The band comes from a score between 1 and 100, worked out from the size of the home, how it is built and insulated, how it is heated and how hot water is provided. The certificate also gives an environmental impact rating based on carbon dioxide emissions, estimated energy costs and a potential rating if the recommended improvements were made.

EPC energy efficiency bands and SAP points
BandSAP pointsWhat it usually means
A92–100Very efficient. Usually new homes with heat pumps or solar.
B81–91Typical of homes built since about 2010.
C69–80Good. A well-insulated older home with an efficient boiler.
D55–68The most common band for Scottish homes.
E39–54Poor insulation or older heating.
F21–38Often electric heating with little insulation.
G1–20Very inefficient and expensive to heat.

Every Scottish EPC comes with a recommendations report. It lists improvements such as loft or wall insulation, a new boiler or heating controls, with an indication of cost and how much each could move the rating. You are not obliged to carry any of them out.

When you need an EPC in Scotland

  • Letting a home. You need a valid EPC before the property is advertised, and you must make it available to anyone interested in renting it.
  • Selling a home. Most sales need a Home Report, which includes the EPC. If a sale is exempt from the Home Report, you still need an EPC before marketing.
  • New homes and conversions. A new building needs an EPC when it is completed.

Three rules catch people out in Scotland:

  • The EPC rating must appear in any advert for the sale or let, including online listings.
  • The EPC must be fixed somewhere in the property. The meter cupboard or beside the boiler is the usual place.
  • Your council can issue a penalty charge of £500 if a home is marketed without a valid EPC.

You can check whether a home already has a valid EPC by searching the address on the Scottish EPC Register. If yours is less than 10 years old and nothing major has changed, you may not need a new one, although a new survey can show improvements the old one missed.

How long an EPC lasts

A current Scottish EPC is valid for 10 years. From 30 April 2028 the Scottish Government is introducing new-style EPCs with three headline ratings, valid for five years. There is a one-year transition for existing certificates. Our guide to the 2028 changes sets out the dates.

What happens on the survey

An accredited domestic energy assessor visits and records the details the rating is calculated from. We need to see every room, the boiler and heating controls, the hot water cylinder if there is one, and the loft if it can be reached safely. Most visits take under an hour.

  1. We measure the depth of loft insulation, or note a room in the roof. Topping a loft up to 270mm is one of the cheapest ways to move up a band.

  2. Wall type and thickness tell us whether walls are solid stone, cavity or timber frame, and whether they have been insulated. Glasgow and Edinburgh sandstone often has no insulation at all.

  3. We record the glazing type and roughly when it was fitted, and how much of each wall is glass.

  4. The make and model let us look up the boiler's tested efficiency. An older non-condensing boiler can cost a home a whole band.

  5. Room thermostat, programmer and radiator valves all count. Missing controls are a quick win in the recommendations.

  6. If there is a cylinder, we check its insulation jacket or foam and whether it has a thermostat.

  7. We count fixed light fittings and how many are low-energy. LEDs throughout are worth having.

  8. Solar PV, solar water heating and heat pumps are recorded with their size. Renewables lift both the rating and the potential.

  9. In Scotland the EPC must be fixed somewhere in the property, often in the meter cupboard or beside the boiler. We remind you where it should go.

  1. Loft tanks should have a tight lid, be insulated and stay below 20°C. We look for debris, rust and signs the water sits still.

  2. Stored hot water should reach 60°C. We check the thermostat setting and record the temperature at the nearest tap.

  3. With a combi there is no stored hot water, which lowers the risk. We record the system type and how hot water reaches each outlet.

  4. Showers create the fine spray that carries legionella. We check the head and hose for scale and whether the shower is used.

  5. We run hot and cold taps and measure the temperature. Hot should reach 50°C within a minute and cold should be below 20°C within two minutes.

  6. Taps in spare rooms or empty flats let water stagnate. We note them so they can be flushed weekly when the property is empty.

  7. Pipework that leads to an outlet that was taken out holds still water. Redundant pipes and dead legs should be removed.

  8. Outside taps and hoses can hold warm stagnant water in summer. We note them and give simple flushing advice.

Things that help on the day

  • Access to the loft hatch and the boiler cupboard.
  • Paperwork for anything we cannot see: cavity wall insulation guarantees, receipts or certificates for loft or wall insulation, window installation dates, solar panel or heat pump certificates.
  • A note of any extensions or rooms in the roof, with roughly when they were built.

Evidence matters. Without it the assessor has to use default assumptions for the age of the home, which can understate the rating.

After the visit

We produce the EPC and lodge it on the Scottish EPC Register, normally within 24 hours of the visit. You get the certificate and recommendations report by email, ready to send to your agent or add to your listing.

Improving your rating

The quickest wins in a Scottish home are usually loft insulation, filling empty cavity walls, upgrading an old boiler, adding heating controls and low-energy lighting. Older sandstone tenements are harder to improve because the walls are solid and many sit in conservation areas. Your recommendations report lists the changes that apply to your home.

Domestic EPC questions

Who can produce an EPC in Scotland?

Only an assessor who belongs to an approved organisation can produce and lodge a Scottish EPC. Every EPC we issue is produced by an accredited domestic energy assessor and lodged on the Scottish EPC Register.

Do I need a new EPC for each new tenant?

No. One valid EPC covers every new let during its 10-year life. You need a new one when it expires before you market the property again.

Can tenants be in the property during the survey?

Yes. Let them know we will need to see every room and the boiler, and lift the loft hatch if there is one.

Does a listed building need an EPC?

Yes. Unlike in England and Wales, listed and historic buildings in Scotland have no general exemption, so they need an EPC when sold or let like any other home. Many improvements that raise the rating need consent in listed buildings and conservation areas, so check with your council before making changes.
