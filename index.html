Every home in Scotland that is sold or let needs an EPC. It rates the home from A to G, shows what it costs to run, and lists the changes that would make it warmer and cheaper to heat.

What each band means

Select a band to see the kind of home that usually sits there. Your own rating comes from the survey: the assessor records the construction, insulation, heating and hot water, then the rating is calculated and lodged on the Scottish EPC Register.

About domestic EPCs

A

Band A, 92–100 points

Very efficient. Usually new homes with heat pumps, solar panels and high levels of insulation.

B

Band B, 81–91 points

Typical of homes built since about 2010, with insulated walls, good glazing and efficient heating.

C

Band C, 69–80 points

Good. Often an older home that has been well insulated and has a modern boiler and controls.

D

Band D, 55–68 points

The most common band for homes in Scotland, including many tenement flats and post-war houses.

E

Band E, 39–54 points

Usually some missing insulation, an older boiler or electric storage heating.

F

Band F, 21–38 points

Often electric heating with little insulation, or solid walls with single glazing.

G

Band G, 1–20 points

Very inefficient and expensive to heat. Usually several major improvements are possible.

See what we check in your home

The same visit seen two ways. Switch between the EPC survey and the legionella assessment, or let it walk you through each point.

  1. We measure the depth of loft insulation, or note a room in the roof. Topping a loft up to 270mm is one of the cheapest ways to move up a band.

  2. Wall type and thickness tell us whether walls are solid stone, cavity or timber frame, and whether they have been insulated. Glasgow and Edinburgh sandstone often has no insulation at all.

  3. We record the glazing type and roughly when it was fitted, and how much of each wall is glass.

  4. The make and model let us look up the boiler's tested efficiency. An older non-condensing boiler can cost a home a whole band.

  5. Room thermostat, programmer and radiator valves all count. Missing controls are a quick win in the recommendations.

  6. If there is a cylinder, we check its insulation jacket or foam and whether it has a thermostat.

  7. We count fixed light fittings and how many are low-energy. LEDs throughout are worth having.

  8. Solar PV, solar water heating and heat pumps are recorded with their size. Renewables lift both the rating and the potential.

  9. In Scotland the EPC must be fixed somewhere in the property, often in the meter cupboard or beside the boiler. We remind you where it should go.

  1. Loft tanks should have a tight lid, be insulated and stay below 20°C. We look for debris, rust and signs the water sits still.

  2. Stored hot water should reach 60°C. We check the thermostat setting and record the temperature at the nearest tap.

  3. With a combi there is no stored hot water, which lowers the risk. We record the system type and how hot water reaches each outlet.

  4. Showers create the fine spray that carries legionella. We check the head and hose for scale and whether the shower is used.

  5. We run hot and cold taps and measure the temperature. Hot should reach 50°C within a minute and cold should be below 20°C within two minutes.

  6. Taps in spare rooms or empty flats let water stagnate. We note them so they can be flushed weekly when the property is empty.

  7. Pipework that leads to an outlet that was taken out holds still water. Redundant pipes and dead legs should be removed.

  8. Outside taps and hoses can hold warm stagnant water in summer. We note them and give simple flushing advice.

Why water temperature matters

Legionella bacteria live naturally in water. They become a risk when water sits at the wrong temperature and is then breathed in as fine spray, usually from a shower. Keeping hot water hot and cold water cold is the main control in a home.

  • Hot water stored at 60°C or above
  • Hot water at least 50°C at the tap within a minute
  • Cold water below 20°C within two minutes

EPCs are changing in 2028

From 30 April 2028 Scotland moves to a new style of EPC with three headline ratings instead of one, and new certificates will last five years instead of ten.

An EPC you get now stays valid for its full 10 years for most purposes, and can still be used to sell or let during a one-year transition. If you are letting or selling in the next 18 months, there is no reason to wait.

Read the 2028 guide

  1. TodayIn force

    Current EPCs

    RdSAP survey, one A to G energy efficiency rating, valid for 10 years. Needed before you market a home for sale or let.

  2. 31 October 2026In force

    Register fee rise

    The fee for lodging an EPC on the Scottish EPC Register went up to fund the new digital register and methodology.

  3. 30 April 2028Confirmed

    New-style EPCs only

    Three headline ratings: Heat Retention, Heating System and Energy Cost. New EPCs are valid for five years and come with a property report.

  4. 30 April 2028 to 30 April 2029Confirmed

    One-year transition

    An old-style EPC under 10 years old can still be used to sell or let. If the property is sold or let again in that year, a new-style EPC is needed.

  5. From 30 April 2029Confirmed

    Old EPCs retired

    Only new-style EPCs can be used for a sale or a new let.

  6. ProposedProposed

    Minimum standard for private lets

    Draft plans would require private rented homes to reach Heat Retention Rating band C, first at a change of tenancy and later for all lets. The timetable has not been confirmed.

Do you cover my area?

We cover the whole of Scotland, from Glasgow and Edinburgh to Aberdeen, Dundee, Inverness, the Borders and the islands. Tell us the postcode and we will confirm the price and a date.

How long does an EPC last in Scotland?

A current Scottish EPC is valid for 10 years. From 30 April 2028 new-style EPCs will be valid for five years. Existing EPCs can still be used during a one-year transition to 30 April 2029, with some limits.

Do I need an EPC to rent out a property in Scotland?

Yes. You need a valid EPC before the property is advertised, the rating must appear in adverts, and you must give a copy to the tenant. In Scotland the EPC must also be fixed somewhere in the property, such as the meter cupboard or beside the boiler. Councils can issue a £500 penalty for a home without one.

Do Scottish landlords need a legionella risk assessment?

Yes. The Scottish Government's Repairing Standard guidance says landlords must have the water system assessed for legionella before a property is first let, and review the assessment at intervals of no more than two years.

Is there such a thing as a legionella certificate?

No. The law asks for a risk assessment, not a certificate or a water test. You get a written assessment that records what was checked, the temperatures found and any actions needed. Water sampling is not normally needed in a home.
