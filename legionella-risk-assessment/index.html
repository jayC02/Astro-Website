Legionella risk assessments for Scottish landlords

Every private let in Scotland needs its water system assessed for legionella before the first tenancy, and reviewed at least every two years. We do it in one short visit and send you a clear written assessment.

Updated 18 November 2026 by the Scotland EPC team at Scottish Energy Services

In short

Every private let in Scotland needs its water system assessed for legionella before the first tenancy, then reviewed at least every two years. There is no "legionella certificate": what you need is a written risk assessment. Ours start from £65 + VAT, or £120 with an EPC at the same visit.

What the law requires in Scotland

Landlords have a duty under health and safety law to assess and control the risk of tenants being exposed to legionella. In Scotland this is spelled out in the Scottish Government's statutory guidance on the Repairing Standard, which private landlords must have regard to. It says:

  • all water systems must be assessed for legionella risk before the property is first let;
  • the risk assessment should be reviewed at intervals of no more than two years, and a new one carried out if anything significant has changed;
  • to help control the risk, landlords should flush the system before letting if this has not been done in the previous two years, make sure cold water tanks have a tight-fitting lid, set stored hot water to at least 60°C, and remove redundant pipework and dead ends.

The same guidance applies to short-term lets, which must also meet the Repairing Standard. The Health and Safety Executive (HSE) enforces the underlying duty.

There is no such thing as a legionella certificate. The law asks for a risk assessment. Water sampling or laboratory testing is not normally needed in a home, and a test on its own does not show the risk is being managed.

Can I do it myself?

You can, if you are competent to do so. HSE guidance accepts that many landlords of simple domestic systems assess the risk themselves. Many landlords and letting agents prefer an independent assessment because it is quick, it measures temperatures properly, and it gives them a record they can show a tenant, an agent or the First-tier Tribunal if the Repairing Standard is ever questioned.

What we check

  1. Loft insulation

    We measure the depth of loft insulation, or note a room in the roof. Topping a loft up to 270mm is one of the cheapest ways to move up a band.

  2. Walls

    Wall type and thickness tell us whether walls are solid stone, cavity or timber frame, and whether they have been insulated. Glasgow and Edinburgh sandstone often has no insulation at all.

  3. Windows

    We record the glazing type and roughly when it was fitted, and how much of each wall is glass.

  4. Boiler

    The make and model let us look up the boiler's tested efficiency. An older non-condensing boiler can cost a home a whole band.

  5. Heating controls

    Room thermostat, programmer and radiator valves all count. Missing controls are a quick win in the recommendations.

  6. Hot water cylinder

    If there is a cylinder, we check its insulation jacket or foam and whether it has a thermostat.

  7. Lighting

    We count fixed light fittings and how many are low-energy. LEDs throughout are worth having.

  8. Solar panels

    Solar PV, solar water heating and heat pumps are recorded with their size. Renewables lift both the rating and the potential.

  9. Meter cupboard

    In Scotland the EPC must be fixed somewhere in the property, often in the meter cupboard or beside the boiler. We remind you where it should go.

  10. Cold water tank

    Loft tanks should have a tight lid, be insulated and stay below 20°C. We look for debris, rust and signs the water sits still.

  11. Hot water cylinder

    Stored hot water should reach 60°C. We check the thermostat setting and record the temperature at the nearest tap.

  12. Combi or system boiler

    With a combi there is no stored hot water, which lowers the risk. We record the system type and how hot water reaches each outlet.

  13. Shower

    Showers create the fine spray that carries legionella. We check the head and hose for scale and whether the shower is used.

  14. Kitchen tap

    We run hot and cold taps and measure the temperature. Hot should reach 50°C within a minute and cold should be below 20°C within two minutes.

  15. Little-used basin

    Taps in spare rooms or empty flats let water stagnate. We note them so they can be flushed weekly when the property is empty.

  16. Dead leg

    Pipework that leads to an outlet that was taken out holds still water. Redundant pipes and dead legs should be removed.

  17. Outside tap

    Outside taps and hoses can hold warm stagnant water in summer. We note them and give simple flushing advice.

On every visit we:

  • identify the type of hot and cold water system, from a simple combi boiler to a cylinder fed by a loft tank;
  • measure water temperatures at the taps, the cylinder and any cold water tank;
  • inspect tanks for lids, insulation, debris and rust;
  • look for dead legs, redundant pipework and outlets that are rarely used;
  • check showers, mixer valves and outside taps;
  • note anything about the tenants or the use of the property that raises the risk, such as long empty periods.

The temperatures that matter

Temperature is the main control in a home. Legionella bacteria multiply in water between 20°C and 45°C, are dormant below 20°C and do not survive above 60°C. Drag the dial to see what each reading means.

Most homes with a combi boiler and no stored water come out as low risk with few or no actions. Homes with loft tanks, hot water cylinders, long pipe runs or periods standing empty are where we most often find something to fix.

Keeping the risk low between assessments

  • Run little-used outlets

    Run taps and showers that are not used often for a couple of minutes once a week, especially in empty properties.

  • Flush before a new tenant

    Flush the system before a new tenant moves in if the property has stood empty.

  • Descale shower heads

    Clean and descale shower heads regularly.

  • Keep the cylinder at 60°C

    Keep the cylinder thermostat at 60°C and ask tenants not to turn it down.

  • Remove dead legs

    Have redundant pipework removed when other plumbing work is being done.

Legionella questions

How often does a legionella risk assessment need to be done in Scotland?

Before a property is first let, then reviewed at intervals of no more than two years under the Repairing Standard guidance. Review it sooner if the water system changes, for example a new boiler or cylinder, or if the property has stood empty.

Is a property with a combi boiler still covered?

Yes. A combi boiler with no stored water is usually low risk, but the duty to assess still applies. The assessment confirms the system type, checks temperatures and looks at showers and little-used outlets.

Do HMOs need a legionella risk assessment?

Yes. HMOs often have larger or shared systems with storage tanks, so the assessment takes longer. Tell us about the property and we will quote.

What is Legionnaires' disease?

A potentially fatal form of pneumonia caused by breathing in tiny water droplets containing legionella bacteria. People over 45, smokers and those with weakened immune systems are at higher risk. It is not caught by drinking water.
